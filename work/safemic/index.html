Inclusive Design / UX Research / Front-End Prototype

SafeMic

Designing a safer voice-chat experience for online gaming, where support feels private, customizable, and controlled by the targeted player.

Snapshot

A Discord-style safety add-on for harmful voice chat.

Project

Inclusive design, UX research, interaction design, front-end prototyping

Duration

One academic quarter

Team

Four designers

Tools

React, Vite, Figma, Word, video editing tools

My role

I connected partner research to an interactive prototype.

I led the partner interview and co-design session, gathered feedback across prototype versions, translated insights into safety settings and action choices, built the React/Vite prototype, recorded demonstrations, and contributed to the final walkthrough and reflection.

Research insight

The harm started when voice revealed identity.

Our co-design partner described being treated normally in a game until she spoke and other players realized she was a woman. The problem was not her skill; it was the gender stereotyping that followed.

This showed us that a report button alone was not enough. Reporting often happens after harm occurs, while players need support during the interaction itself.

Design shift

From automatic punishment to player-controlled support.

01

Early concept

A separate moderation dashboard that automatically detected and muted harmful speech.

02

Partner feedback

Safety should not mean the system makes every decision, especially with friends or jokes.

03

Final direction

A Discord-style add-on with filter categories, sensitivity levels, trusted friends, and private action choices.

Final interaction

SafeMic helps players respond without public confrontation.

Customize

Choose detection categories, sensitivity, automatic muting, friends, and channels.

Detect

When harmful speech is flagged, SafeMic privately explains what was detected.

Decide

The targeted player can continue, extend mute time, report, block, mark incorrect detection, or adjust filters.

Outcome

The final prototype communicated a complete safety flow.

The Discord-style version felt more realistic to our partner than the original dashboard. It also showed potential for supporting other users targeted in voice-based spaces, including minors and marginalized players.

The prototype remains conceptual: it does not connect to Discord, process live audio, or solve complex detection issues such as tone, sarcasm, accents, or multilingual speech. The next step would be broader testing with gaming groups.

Reflection

Protection without agency can still feel controlling.

This project shifted my thinking from “What should the system do to protect the user?” to “How can the system support the user in deciding what should happen next?” It helped me practice sensitive interviewing, co-design facilitation, interaction prototyping, and communicating a speculative safety system through a clear user flow.
